What this course is built on

Not goal-setting theory. The thesis, in one paragraph

A SMART objective is not a scoring trick. It is the load-bearing sentence of the proposal: the place where the call topic, the science, the work plan and the impact claim all have to agree with each other in one breath. Written well it holds the whole structure up. Written badly it is the first visible sign that the specification underneath is thin — and the specification underneath is exactly what decides whether you are funded.

One idea to carry in

A proposal is a chain, and every link has a name that evaluators use precisely even when applicants use them loosely: the call's expected outcomes, the project result, the objectives, the indicators, the work packages, the deliverables, and finally the outcomes and impact. That chain is the golden thread, and Module 2 is devoted entirely to it.

The objective sits in the middle on purpose. It is the only link that faces both ways — upward to the call and the result, downward to the indicators and the work. That is why the whole course is organised around it.

What 59 proposals actually show

What we did

We took 59 proposals this office has submitted, pulled the evaluation report the Commission sent back for each one, and compared the 22 that were funded against the 37 that were not. Then we read every sentence the evaluators wrote — nearly 2,800 of them — and counted what they praised and what they picked apart.

We also went back to the proposal documents themselves and scored how each one was built, including how its objectives were written.

Everything in this module is a count from that work. Where the numbers say something surprising — and several of them do — the surprise is in the numbers, not in how we read them.

Finding one: excellence decides

Start with the score sheet. Across all 59 proposals the funded and rejected groups are separated by 0.85 points on Excellence, 0.55 on Impact — and 0.12 on Quality and efficiency of the implementation. Twelve hundredths of a point. If all you knew about a proposal was its implementation score, you would have almost no idea whether it was funded.

That is one way of looking at it. Here is a second, from a completely different angle: where the criticism lands.

Look at what that inversion means in practice. Open a winning ESR and nearly half of everything the evaluator complained about is in the implementation section — the work plan, the risk table, the effort distribution. Open a losing one and nearly half of it is in the science.

A third check agrees. In losing proposals, Excellence is the lowest of the three sub-scores in 22 of 33 cases. And not one of the 22 funded proposals scored below 4.0 on Excellence — not one — while three funded proposals carried an Implementation score below 4.0 and one carried an Impact of 3.5. Excellence is a gate. The other two are trades.

Compensation, and its one direction

Four funded proposals carry a visibly weak criterion. WON Label2Enable scored 3.5 on Implementation and was funded on the back of a perfect 5.0 Excellence. WON i2X scored 3.5 on Implementation with 5.0 on Relevance. WON XpanDH scored 3.5 on Impact, carried by Excellence 4.0 and Implementation 4.5. The fourth, WON GDI, took an Implementation of 3.0 with 4.0 on both other criteria — and was the only proposal submitted under its topic, which is a different mechanism altogether.

What matters is what is absent: not once did a proposal compensate for weak Excellence with strong Implementation. The trade only runs one way.

Finding two: the losses are not what you think

Before drawing any lesson from the rejected group, split it. Twenty-one of the 37 losses — 57% — cleared their score threshold and were rejected on ranking and budget, not on quality. Six more were stage-1 or EIC evaluations with no comparable threshold. That leaves ten proposals where the ESR describes a quality failure.

Finding three: the two ladders

Here is the chart this whole course turns on. It shows two things at once: how often evaluators criticised the objectives, and how often they criticised the methodology, across the three populations.

Read the green bars first, and read them honestly, because they say something that looks like bad news for a course about objectives. Objectives are criticised in 32% of winning ESRs and 29% of near-misses. That is not a difference. At the margin — between a funded bid and one that cleared the bar and lost on rank — how the objectives were written separates nothing at all.

Then look at where it does move: 50% in the sub-threshold group. Weak objectives are associated with falling through the floor, not with losing at the margin.

Now the blue bars. Methodology criticism runs 45% → 90% → 100%. That gap between funded and near-miss — 45 points — is the widest of any of the 34 codes in the analysis. Method specification is what decides at the margin.

So why spend the best part of a day on objectives?

Because the two bars are not independent. The discipline that makes an objective measurable — naming a threshold, a baseline, a validation method, an owner and a date — is the same discipline the methodology needs one level down. An objective that says "develop and integrate digital components" cannot be followed by a method section that specifies anything, because nothing has been committed to. The objective is where under-specification becomes visible, and it is the cheapest place to fix it: it is four sentences, not forty pages.

Put plainly: writing SMART objectives will not by itself move you from 12.5 to 13.5. Writing them properly is how you find out, in week two rather than month six, that your method is not yet specified.

Carry these four sentences into the rest of the course

Module 2 · The golden thread

The golden thread

An objective is never judged alone. It is judged against the call above it and the work below it. This module is the whole chain — what each link is, who is responsible for it, and the two tables that let you check both joins in an afternoon.

50 minutesTwo worked tablesOne exercise, one knowledge check

By the end of this module you will

  • be able to name every link in the chain and say whose job each one is
  • use objective, result, outcome, impact, indicator, deliverable and milestone correctly and separately
  • be able to apply the handover test that separates an objective from an outcome
  • know why nothing can be judged without the call text in front of you
  • be able to build a call-mapping table and a traceability matrix, and read both for what is missing

The chain

Call: expected outcomes→ Project result→ Objectives→ Indicators→ Work packages→ Deliverables→ Outcomes & impact

Read it left to right and it is a plan. Read it right to left and it is an audit: every impact you claim has to trace back through an outcome, a result, an indicator, an objective and something the call asked for. Anything that cannot make that trip is decoration, and evaluators spot decoration quickly.

The objective sits in the middle on purpose. It is the only link facing both ways — up to the call and the result, down to the indicators and the work. Which is why an objective is never wrong on its own. It is wrong relative to something: the call above it, or the work below it.

The vocabulary that costs people points

Seven words. Evaluators use them precisely. Applicants use them interchangeably, and then get told their impact pathway is unclear when the real problem is that they called an activity an objective.

TermWhat it isTest that separates it from its neighbour
ObjectiveSomething the project will have achieved by the end, under its own controlCould the consortium be held to it at the final review? If not, it is an outcome or an impact.
ResultThe thing that exists at the end — the model, the framework, the dataset, the guidelineCould you put it on a table, or point at a URL? A result is a noun.
OutcomeThe change in what other people do with your result, shortly after the projectRequires somebody outside the consortium to act. You cause it; you do not control it.
ImpactThe wider, later effect — economic, societal, scientificHappens after the project ends and is only partly attributable to you.
IndicatorThe specific quantity you will observe to show an objective or outcome was reachedIt has a unit and a way of measuring it. "Uptake" is not an indicator; "number of hospitals with the tool in routine use, from the deployment register" is.
DeliverableA document or artefact submitted to the Commission on a dateIt has a number, a lead partner and a month. D3.2 is a deliverable; "a validated model" is a result.
MilestoneA control point where you check whether you can proceedIt has a pass condition. If nothing is decided by reaching it, it is a deadline, not a milestone.

The line that matters is between objective and outcome, because that is where responsibility changes hands. You can be held to an objective at the final review. You cannot be held to an outcome, because it needs somebody outside the consortium to act. Evaluators know this, and they penalise proposals that blur it in a specific way: an objective that is really an outcome reads as an objective the consortium cannot deliver — a credibility failure, scored under Excellence, that started as a wording problem.

The handover test

Read your objective and ask: if every partner does exactly what the work plan says and nobody outside the consortium changes their behaviour, is this objective achieved?

Yes → it is an objective. No → it is an outcome, and it belongs in the impact section with a pathway underneath it.

"Improve early detection of heart failure across European primary care" fails the test. "Deliver a validated risk score with regulatory-grade evidence and secure adoption commitments from four national primary-care networks" passes it — and the improvement in detection is then the outcome it causes.

Where the chain starts: the call text

The first link is not yours. It is the call's, and it governs everything downstream. Here is the sentence from the evaluators' own scoring form, with the clause that matters in bold:

"The following aspects will be taken into account, to the extent that the proposed work corresponds to the description in the work programme: Clarity and pertinence of the project's objectives, and the extent to which the proposed work is ambitious, and goes beyond the state of the art."

Evaluation form (HE RIA and IA) v4.0, criterion 1 — Excellence

Not "to the extent that the work is good". To the extent that it corresponds to the work programme. Relevance is not one test among several — it is the condition under which the others are applied at all. An objective that is clear, measurable, credible and dated but answers a question the call did not ask scores nothing, and there is no partial credit for excellence in the wrong direction.

Non-negotiable

You cannot assess an objective without the call topic text open beside it. Not the topic title. The full text: expected outcomes, scope, and any specific conditions. Every exercise in this course supplies one. Every prompt in the generator in Module 8 demands one as its first input. A review that skips this step is not a review — it is a proofread.

Here is what it looks like when the call is treated as background reading. PIPER cleared its threshold comfortably at 13.5 and still lost:

"The proposal does not satisfactorily respond to the ambition of expected activities in the call topic: 'develop mechanisms and technologies for IPR-aware data manipulation, including reviewing best practices in anonymisation / pseudonymisation techniques and synthetic data generation'."

NEAR-MISS PIPER — Excellence 3.5, total 13.5

The evaluator quoted the call topic back at the applicant. That sentence is the call text, pasted into the evaluation report as the standard the proposal failed to meet. Which is exactly why the call text is the first thing on the desk.

The partial row is silent coverage: the requirement is answered, in a task, on a later page — but not in the objectives, so relevance is judged before the evaluator reaches it. That is precisely what happened to PIPER above.

The uncovered row is worse, and commoner than people expect: an expected outcome with two halves, one of which quietly went unanswered. Splitting compound requirements into separate rows is the only reliable way to catch it. If a row reads "X and Y", make it two rows.

How to use the table honestly

Not every row has to be filled. Some calls ask for more than one project can carry, and deciding not to answer something is legitimate. What is not legitimate is not knowing. An empty row is a decision — make it deliberately, then either add an objective or write the sentence that explains why this project addresses the rest.

The one thing at the impact end worth your time

Of everything judged under Impact, one thing separates funded proposals from near-miss rejections: the pathway from result to outcome — criticised in 32% of funded proposals and 48% of near-misses. Nothing else under Impact separates the two groups at all.

That pathway is precisely the right-hand columns of the matrix above: what exists at the end, who picks it up, what makes them, and how you would know.

One gap comes up again and again in proposals from this office: exploitation and uptake left as intent — named target actors with no evidence anybody has committed. "We will engage industry" is intent. "Four MoUs countersigned by M30, template in Annex" is evidence. The difference is one cell in the matrix, and it is the cell that turns an outcome claim into a traceable one.

What SMART means in a proposal

The acronym you learned in a management course is not the standard you are being marked against. The real one is shorter, it is printed in the application form, and evaluators answer it back to you word for word.

The standard, as printed

Open the Horizon Europe Part B application form at section 1.1 and this is what it asks you, word for word:

"Briefly describe the objectives of your proposed work. Why are they pertinent to the work programme topic? Are they measurable and verifiable? Are they realistically achievable?"

Horizon Europe standard application form, Part B, section 1.1 Objectives and ambition

Three questions. Four tests, because "measurable and verifiable" is really two. Map them onto the acronym you already know and there are no surprises:

SMARTThe words actually usedThe question being asked
Specific"clarity" · "clear"Can a stranger tell exactly what you will have produced?
Measurable"measurable and verifiable"Is there a stated way to check whether you did it — and something to check it against?
Achievable"realistically achievable"Given this consortium, this budget and this duration, is it credible?
Relevant"pertinent to the work programme topic"Does it serve this call's expected outcomes, not your research interests?
Time-bound"within the project's time-frame"By when, and is the order in which things happen actually possible?

The single most useful thing in this module

Evaluators answer the application form's questions back to you, in the form's order, using the form's words. Once you can see that, an evaluation report stops being prose and becomes a mark sheet.

Watch it happen

Here is the objectives verdict on a proposal that was rejected. It looks like two sentences of prose. It is actually four answers:

"The objectives are partially clear and pertinent to the call topic. They are partially measurable and verifiable, and not realistically achievable."

LOST YOUNG4LIFE — Excellence 2.50, total 11.5 against a threshold of 12

Clear? Partially. Pertinent? Partially. Measurable and verifiable? Partially. Realistically achievable? No. Four questions, four answers, in the form's own order — and an Excellence score of 2.5, the lowest we recorded in a recent bid.

Now the same four slots on a proposal that was funded:

"The project objectives are clearly formulated, very well aligned and relevant to the topic, realistically achievable, measurable and verifiable."

WON CANDLE — Excellence 4.0, total 13.0

And one in between, where you can watch the evaluator hedge slot by slot:

"They are partially measurable, partially verifiable, and sufficiently achievable for the core scientific components. However, some success criteria and validation milestones … are not sufficiently operationalised."

LOST AERO MIND — Excellence 3.5, total 11.0 against a threshold of 12

Note what the evaluator did there: "sufficiently achievable for the core scientific components". They are not marking the objectives as a block. They are walking down the list and marking each one.

Measurable does not mean numeric

This is where most training goes wrong, and the evidence is unambiguous.

Look again at CANDLE, which was told its objectives were "measurable and verifiable". Here is its second objective, complete and unedited:

Won · CANDLE · Excellence 4.0

O2. Identify challenges and barriers at the national and EU levels, including technical, regulatory, operational, and ELSI-related barriers, that may hinder the implementation of UNCAN.eu and ECPDC platforms. Propose operational solutions to overcome these barriers, ensuring that NCDNs support compliance with the European Health Data Space (EHDS) infrastructures for the primary and secondary use of data.

Not a single number in it. No target, no percentage, no date. And it was rated measurable and verifiable.

Why? Because every noun in it is checkable. There is a defined set of barriers, at defined levels, across four named categories. There is a named thing to produce — operational solutions. There is a named standard to check compliance against — EHDS. At the end of the project you can put the document on the table and ask: are the barriers identified, at both levels, in all four categories? Are the solutions operational? Do they support EHDS compliance? Every question has an answer.

Now the contrast. YOUNG4LIFE, also with no numbers:

Lost · YOUNG4LIFE · Excellence 2.50

O1. Identify behavioural, social and environmental determinants of lifestyle behaviours among young people, building on European evidence and participatory research approaches.

O3. Develop and integrate digital components, including conversational agents, personalised feedback systems and gamified engagement mechanisms, benefiting from chronic disease management learnings.

O6. Advance technological and social innovation, progressing digital solutions from TRL4 to TRL8 while generating scalable blueprints for youth-centred prevention ecosystems.

Same absence of numbers, completely different fate. Ask the end-of-project question of O1: have you identified the determinants? There is no answer. How many? Which populations? Against what prior evidence? "Identify determinants" is an activity with no finishing line. O6 is worse — it contains a number (TRL4 to TRL8) and is still not verifiable, because TRL8 of what, assessed by whom, against what?

The rule

An objective is measurable when there is a stated way to tell, at the end, whether it happened. A number is the most common way to supply that. A named, checkable thing is the other. What fails is neither: a verb with no finishing line.

And this is why piling numbers into a KPI table does not help. It does not make an objective verifiable; it moves the verification somewhere the evaluator has to go and look for it. Module 4 has the evidence.

Where the five elements sit on the chain

Module 2 put the objective in the middle of the chain, facing both ways. The five elements divide neatly along the same line.

ElementWhich way it facesWhat it is really asking
RelevantUpward, to the callDoes this answer something the topic asked for, in the topic's own terms?
SpecificDownward, to the workIs the thing you will produce named precisely enough that somebody could build it?
MeasurableDownward, to the indicatorsIs there a stated way to check it, and something to check it against?

Which is the practical reason Relevant is assessed differently from the other four. You can judge Specific, Measurable and Time-bound by reading the objective. You cannot judge Relevant without the call text — which is why Module 2 spent an afternoon building a table for it.

S — Specific

The test: a stranger who reads only this one sentence can say what will exist at the end, for whom, and in what setting — without reading the rest of the proposal.

That last clause is the whole difficulty. You know what your objective means. You have been in the meetings. The evaluator has ninety minutes, fourteen other proposals, and no context. Specificity is not about writing more; several of the vaguest objectives we read are the longest. It is about whether the sentence closes.

"For most of the descriptions the provided text lacks explicit explanations … Thus, the specific objectives as provided in the proposal are not sufficiently detailed and clear."

LOST EXPECT — Excellence 3.5, total 11.0

"Some of the objectives and sub-objectives are unclear and lack coherence. For example, the relevance of harmonising multinational databases pertaining to all the 80 ADs is unclear from clinical, epidemiological, and preventive perspectives. This is a significant weakness."

LOST COMET-AD — Excellence 2.0, total 8.0

Note the shape of the last one. The evaluator did not say "vague". They picked one objective, asked what it was for, and could not answer. That is what an unclear objective looks like from the other side of the desk: not confusion, but a question with no answer in the document.

One honest qualification

Demands for more specificity appear in 73% of losing ESRs — and also in 55% of winning ones. Their mere presence tells you little. What discriminates are the harder variants: "lacks concrete or technical detail" occurs five times, every one of them a rejection. "Remains general, generic or aspirational" occurs three times, again every one a rejection. Small numbers, but clean. Meanwhile "it is unclear how…" appears in eight ESRs, six of them wins — an evaluator asking a question is in a different mood from one recording an absence.

What specific looks like when it works

Won · ENT1DEP · Excellence 5.0 · total 15.0

"The objectives are clearly stated and fully aligned with the Call topic, aimed at establishing a causal link between Coxsackieviruses B infection (CVB) and beta cell autoimmunity and its role in the pathogenesis of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D)."

One named pathogen, one named biological mechanism, one named disease, one named relationship to establish. You could disagree with it. That is the point — a specific objective is one that could be wrong.

Won · EDC-NAFLD · Excellence 5.0 · total 15.0 — the objectives as submitted

Objective 1 is to characterise the impact of environmental exposure to EDCs on the internal exposome (metabolome, gut microbiome, epigenome, proteome, immunome) and degree of liver damage in NAFLD in prospective study settings.

Objective 2 is to understand the mechanisms of hepatic injury and resolution due to exposures to EDCs. EDC exposure studies will be performed in murine models of NAFLD, zebrafish models, and 2D/3D in vitro models.

Objective 3 is to develop and implement an integrative systems biology and screening platform for the studies of EDCs in NAFLD.

Look at what each objective carries with it: a named class of exposure, five named omics layers, a named disease, and — crucially — the study setting. Objective 2's "understand the mechanisms" would be family-level on its own; the following sentence drops it two rungs by naming the three model systems. The ESR came back: "The objectives of the proposal are very clear, specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and well aligned with the scope of the call." Perfect 5.0.

M — Measurable

The test: at the final review, is there a stated way to decide whether this objective was met — and something to compare the result against?

Measurable in a proposal has three components. Most objectives supply one of them.

ComponentWhat it answersUsually…
TargetWhat level counts as success?present — this is the bit everybody remembers

The case that proves it: ALMA

This one is worth studying closely, because it is a proposal from this office that did almost everything the textbooks tell you to do.

Lost · ALMA · Excellence 3.5 · total 11.5 — objectives 1 and 3 as submitted, abridged

Objective 1: Set up Living Labs in three pilot regions (Praia (CV), São Domingos (CV), Bissau (GB)).
Measures of success: 3 Living Labs operational by M3 with governance charters … ≥12 co-design sprints by M12 (≥4/region), ≥24 by M24 … ≥300 participants cumulative (≥60% women/youth) … ≥80% of priority UX/safety issues resolved by the next iteration. Related to WP(s): 3, 5

Objective 3: Co-develop and deploy multilingual GenAI triage tools adapted to context.
Measures of success: Triage accuracy ≥70% vs. clinician gold-standard on a stratified test set by M18 … ≥25k unique users completing ≥1 triage by M36 … <5% safety overrides quarterly … ≥40% referral conversion for RED/ORANGE cases … equity: performance parity gap ≤5 p.p. Related to WP(s): 2, 4, 5

Numbers everywhere. Dates on nearly every one. Work packages named against each objective. Definitions supplied for ambiguous terms. And here is what came back:

"However, some of the objectives lack a baseline against which their true achievement can be measured."

NEAR-MISS ALMA — Excellence 3.5

Go back and check it against the three-component test. Triage accuracy ≥70% — compared with what? What is current triage accuracy in these settings? Is 70% an improvement, a regression, or roughly par? Three Living Labs — against zero, or against something already running? ≥300 participants — is that a lot?

Notice which component is missing and which is not. The verification is largely there — "vs. clinician gold-standard on a stratified test set" tells you exactly how accuracy will be measured. What is absent is the reference point. And without it a target is a number, not a measure. A number you cannot compare to anything is not a measurement.

The repair is one clause per objective. "Triage accuracy ≥70% against clinician gold standard, up from the 52% achieved by the existing paper protocol in the Praia baseline audit (M4)." Same target, same verification — now it means something.

What "broadly defined" gets you

"However, the key performance indicators are broadly defined, which limits the measurability and verifiability of the objectives."

NEAR-MISS Multi-Optima — Excellence 4.0, total 12.5

"The objectives are supported by realistic targets, but they are defined in generic terms (e.g., in terms of total number of tools, total number of interviews)."

NEAR-MISS PIPER — Excellence 3.5, total 13.5

Read that second one twice. The proposal had targets. The targets were realistic. And they still failed the test, because counting interviews measures effort, not achievement. "Twelve interviews" is a description of what you will spend; it says nothing about what you will know afterwards. Evaluators see through activity metrics immediately, and they are one of the most common ways a proposal manages to look quantified while measuring nothing.

"However, relevant and quantified indicators specifically linked to each objective to enable measurement of their achievement are insufficiently considered."

WON suHCO — Excellence 4.0, total 13.0

That one is from a funded proposal, and the phrase that matters is "specifically linked to each objective". The indicators existed. They were not attached to the objectives they were supposed to measure.

Placement beats volume

The counter-intuitive finding, and it is well evidenced

KPI density — the number of KPI mentions per thousand words — correlates negatively with the ESR total (rho −0.24) and with the Excellence sub-score (rho −0.26). Adding KPI text does not buy score. Meanwhile, proposals that place the threshold, the date and the owner inside the objective sentence are consistently rated stronger on objectives than proposals holding the same numbers in a separate table.

WON Label2Scale and NEAR-MISS CARDIORISE put threshold, date and owner in the objective text and were rated STRONG on objectives, at Excellence 5.0 and 4.5.

Why would placement matter? Because a KPI table is read once, quickly, at the end of a section. The objective is read at the top of the Excellence assessment, when the evaluator is forming the judgement they will spend the rest of the section confirming.

And a warning about what a dense KPI table buys you

LOST PREDICT carried the densest KPI apparatus of any proposal in the recent batch. Here is what its ESR said about the objectives:

"The project's objectives are clear and pertinent to the call topic. The project's objectives are sufficiently measurable, sufficiently verifiable, and sufficiently achievable."

LOST PREDICT — Excellence 3.5, total 11.0 against a threshold of 12

The objectives passed. And the Excellence score was still 3.5, because the methodology underneath them was at family level. Two things to take from that.

First, and this is the honest version of the whole course: good objectives do not rescue a thin method. They are where you find out the method is thin.

Second, note the word the evaluator reached for three times. "Sufficiently" reads as approval and functions as a ceiling — it is the register of a reviewer confirming a box is ticked rather than one who is impressed. In the 59 proposals "adequately", its close cousin, appears in 84% of losing ESRs and 41% of winning ones. That is a reading aid for an ESR when it arrives, not something to look for in a draft: these are the evaluator's words, and their frequency in proposal text has no relationship to score at all.

Where AERO MIND went, and it is instructive

AERO MIND did put its measures inside the objectives, and even supplied baselines where they were genuinely absent — "baseline: no AP-specific EU CoI estimates exist at project start", "baseline: 0 — no comparable composite indicator exists". That is textbook. It still came back:

"They are partially measurable, partially verifiable, and sufficiently achievable for the core scientific components. However, some success criteria and validation milestones, including the concordance threshold in the bidirectional validation architecture, are not sufficiently operationalised."

LOST AERO MIND — Excellence 3.5, total 11.0

The measures on the individual components were fine. The measure on the join between two components was missing. When the novelty of a project is that two halves talk to each other, the number that matters is the one that says what successful agreement between them looks like. Four of our nine most recent rejections failed in exactly this way. Module 5 gives it its own pattern.

A — Achievable

The test: given this consortium, this budget and this duration, would a sceptical expert believe you can do it — and is it worth doing?

Achievable is the element people get wrong in both directions, and both directions cost you.

Failure mode one: over-claim

"The stated objectives are vague, overly ambitious, and at times veer into unscientific territory, such as referring to a 'Holy grail objective' or promising to 'solve the problems once and for all.' While the overall goal of developing a functional prototype is appropriate, the intermediate objectives are described in vague terms and lack quantitative metrics or detailed experimental plans."

LOST HEADROOM — total 2.75

That is an EIC Pathfinder bid that took 2.75 out of 5 on its weighted scale, and the evaluator's irritation is audible. But the mechanism is not "they were too ambitious". It is that the ambition was asserted rather than resourced: no metrics, no experimental plan. Ambition that is not accompanied by a plan reads as a lack of plan, not as vision.

"Insufficient details are given to ensure this ambition is credible and achievable."

LOST REALDATA — total 9.0 against a threshold of 10

Notice the word "details". Not "the ambition is too high" — details. The evaluator is not asking you to want less. They are asking you to show the arithmetic.

Failure mode two: under-claim

And here is why "just be conservative" is not the answer:

"The proposed work is poorly ambitious and it does not go beyond the state of the art. … The proposal does not adequately demonstrate the advance beyond the state-of-the-art as it states that no new data, methods or algorithms will be generated. It also states that legislative gaps identified during the work will be highlighted for further action rather than being addressed in the proposed project."

NEAR-MISS PIPER — Excellence 3.5, total 13.5

A safe, deliverable, entirely credible set of objectives — and it cost them Excellence. Achievability is not a licence to promise a literature review. The criterion is "clarity and pertinence of the objectives and the extent to which the proposed work is ambitious, and goes beyond the state of the art". Both halves are in the same bullet.

The shape you are aiming for

An achievable objective is ambitious in what it claims and conservative in what it assumes. It promises a genuine advance, and then shows the advance is reachable by naming the resource that makes it reachable — the cohort you already hold, the platform already at TRL6, the partner who has done it once before at smaller scale.

Failure mode three: declaring instead of demonstrating

This one is subtle and expensive, and there is a perfect example of it. Act4Dem structured its objectives section around exactly the right idea: it labelled every objective P/V/A — pertinent, verifiable, achievable — and wrote a justification paragraph under each. Here is a representative one:

Lost · Act4Dem · Excellence 3.0 · total 8.0 — as submitted

O1 P/V/A: "Facilitating trust in local governance has proven an effective lever for improving trust in the democratic state. Wider deployment and mainstreaming of novel methods/tools for participative SGI innovation governance … is, however, hampered for various reasons. A dedicated effort is therefore required … A project duration of 42 months, availability of all required expertise in the consortium and a balanced budgeting of the required work tasks ensures achievability of the overall project."

Read the last sentence again. "A project duration of 42 months, availability of all required expertise and a balanced budgeting … ensures achievability." That is an assertion that the project is achievable. It contains no information an evaluator can check. And the verdict:

"However, the proposal objectives are only partially formulated as measurable, especially with regard to the quality of governance. For example, generation of robust evidence through formative and summative evaluation activities, or the implementation of evidence-based stakeholder dialogue and mutual learning formats are not convincingly supported by the quantitative evidences. Therefore, the extent to which some of the objectives can be realistically achieved during the project's lifetime does not clearly arise from the proposal."

LOST Act4Dem — Excellence 3.0

"Does not clearly arise from the proposal." The applicant said it was achievable. They did not make it arise. The fix is not more assurance; it is one concrete anchor per objective — a prior result at a stated scale, a named dataset already in hand, a partner with a citable track record at this size.

What evaluators are actually checking

Question in their headWhat satisfies it
Has anyone done anything like this?A named prior project or publication, with the scale it reached
Do these people have the inputs?Named cohorts, datasets, platforms, sites — held, not to be recruited

Answer that last question inside the objective — one clause naming the reduced version you would still deliver if the hard part does not work. The evaluator forms their achievability judgement while reading the objective, not while reading a table forty pages later.

R — Relevant

The test: does this objective serve one of this topic's expected outcomes — and can the evaluator see that in under ten seconds?

Relevance is not a fifth consideration. It is the condition on the other four: "to the extent that the proposed work corresponds to the description in the work programme". Everything else is assessed inside that clause.

Relevance is scored partially, not pass/fail

The most instructive case here is a proposal that was funded:

"In general, the objectives are clear, although not fully pertinent to the topic. For example: (i) the envisioned ICT toolbox is not fully explained, (ii) the objectives do not tackle compliance with specific regulations and directives (such as GDPR and NIS Directive), (iii) the realization of the foreseen public infrastructure is not supported by activities that will ensure its scalability, and (iv) the involvement of specific entities in a Pan-European ecosystem is not clear."

WON XpanDH — funded, total 12.0

They were funded — and they gave away points on four separate sub-clauses of the topic. Each of those four is a thing the call asked for that the objectives did not visibly answer. Relevance leaks a fraction at a time.

And at the other end, it can be fatal on its own:

"The proposed research focuses on autoimmune diseases (AD), which encompass more than 80 pathologies, and therefore is not fully in scope as it does not focus on a single condition. This is a significant weakness."

LOST COMET-AD — Excellence 2.0, total 8.0

That is the first sentence of the Excellence assessment. Everything after it was written by an evaluator who had already decided the proposal was answering a different question.

The technique that works: map, do not assert

Compare how a funded proposal handled the same problem:

"Each of the three call objectives (horizon scanning, sandbox driver analysis, end-to-end recommendations) is mapped onto dedicated, clearly defined pillars (e.g. regulatory insight guiding fitness-criteria development; industry partners supplying R&D pipelines for simulations). … The description of five methodological “pillars” ensures that each objective is logically addressed."

WON BRIDGE — Excellence 4.5, total 13.0. The same ESR: "The objectives of the proposal are very clear and very pertinent."

The evaluator is reciting the applicant's own mapping back at them. That is what you want: you have supplied the sentence they will write.

The instrument that produces it is the call-mapping table from Module 2 — one row per expected outcome and per "should" in the scope, quoted verbatim, with the objective that answers it beside it. Build it before you write the objectives. If you skipped Module 2, this is the point to go back for it, because Relevant is the only element you cannot assess from the objective alone.

Where relevance is actually lost

Two ways, and neither is "we ignored the call".

Scope drift. The objectives cover what the consortium is good at rather than what the topic asked for. Every partner's speciality appears; two of the call's expected outcomes do not. This is the commonest failure in a large consortium and it happens in the room, not on the page.

Silent coverage. You do address the requirement, in work package 5, on page 31. But it is not in the objectives, so the evaluator forms their view of relevance before reaching it. PIPER lost points to a requirement the evaluator quoted verbatim from the call — and that is what a proposal looks like when the answer exists but not where relevance is judged.

T — Time-bound

The test: when is each objective achieved, and is the order in which they are achieved actually possible?

Time-bound is the element that gets the least attention and is the cheapest to fix. It has two halves, and the second is the one that costs real points.

Half one: a date on the objective

"However, as a minor shortcoming, indicators on when the objectives are obtained are not sufficiently included."

WON ASSESS-DHT — Excellence 4.5, total 14.5

Fourteen and a half out of fifteen, and they still lost a fraction on this. "A minor shortcoming" is the evaluator's own label — but at 4.5 rather than 5.0 the fraction is the whole gap. This is the cheapest half-point in the entire course: put a month number in the sentence.

The convention is project months, not calendar dates: by M18, from M24, by M30. Two rules. Never write "by the end of the project" for an objective whose result other work depends on — that is not a date, it is an absence of one. And never date an objective later than the work that needs its output.

Half two: the sequence has to be possible

This is where it stops being cosmetic. Read the objectives as a sequence and ask what must exist before what.

The recurring pattern in the losing proposals is what the analysis calls a late-loaded critical path: validation, integration, ethics closure and exploitation all concentrated in the final year. It shows up as objectives that are all dated M36–M48, or not dated at all, in a project where three of them clearly depend on each other.

What a late-loaded set looks like

  • O1 — develop the model  ·  M1–M36
  • O2 — validate the model in three clinical sites  ·  M30–M42
  • O3 — deliver regulatory-ready evidence package  ·  M42–M48

Every objective has a date. The set is still not credible: validation starts six months before development ends, the sites have to be recruited and ethics-approved inside that window, and the entire regulatory deliverable rests on the last six months with no slack. An experienced evaluator reads that in ten seconds and it colours their judgement of the whole work plan — which is exactly how a Time-bound problem turns into a methodology criticism.

The same three objectives, sequenced

  • O1 — deliver a locked v1 model on the retrospective cohort  ·  by M18, gate for O2
  • O2 — validate v1 prospectively in three sites, ethics approvals secured by M14  ·  M18–M36
  • O3 — deliver the evidence package, drafted from M30 against the v1 results  ·  by M44, four months' contingency

Same science, same duration. What changed is that each objective now names the thing that must be finished before it starts, and the last one has visible slack. That is what "realistically achievable within the project's time-frame" means in practice.

Three questions to run over any objective set

  1. The gate question. For each objective, what has to exist before it can start — and is that thing an earlier objective with an earlier date?
  2. The last-year question. How much of the project's value is created after M36? If it is most of it, the reviewer at the interim will have nothing to look at.
  3. The slack question. Does anything finish in the final month? If so, there is no recovery room, and evaluators know it.

Ten ways objectives go wrong

1 · The activity in objective's clothing

What it looks like: the objective begins with a verb describing work rather than a result. Identify. Explore. Investigate. Analyse. Co-design. Engage. Develop and integrate.

Lost · YOUNG4LIFE · Excellence 2.50

O1 — "Identify behavioural, social and environmental determinants of lifestyle behaviours among young people, building on European evidence and participatory research approaches."

"The objectives are partially clear and pertinent to the call topic. They are partially measurable and verifiable, and not realistically achievable."

Diagnosis. "Identify determinants" has no completion state. There is no moment at which identification is finished, so there is nothing to verify and nothing to date. The proposal has described its method as if it were its goal.

Repair. Ask "and then what?" until you reach a noun somebody outside the project would want. Identify determinants → so that we can rank them → so that the intervention targets the top three. The objective is the ranked, evidenced set of three, not the identifying.

2 · The family-level claim

What it looks like: the objective names a category of method rather than the method. Machine learning approaches. A risk modelling framework. Digital components. Advanced analytics.

"The modelling framework is described at the level of analytical families rather than at the level of implementation, and overall the details provided are insufficient."

LOST PREDICT — Excellence 3.5

Diagnosis. A family name is a promise to decide later. Evaluators read it as evidence that the decision has not been made — which, honestly, it usually has not.

Repair. Name one. If you genuinely cannot commit, name the two candidates and the criterion that will choose between them by a stated month. "We will select between X and Y at M9 on held-out AUC" is specific. "Advanced modelling approaches" is not.

Cost: an afternoon with the technical lead. This is the single highest-value hour in a proposal.

3 · Numbers without a baseline

What it looks like: targets everywhere, nothing to compare them against.

"However, some of the objectives lack a baseline against which their true achievement can be measured."

NEAR-MISS ALMA — Excellence 3.5

Diagnosis. ≥70% accuracy, ≥300 participants, ≥2 SMEs. Every one is a number and not one is a measurement, because none of them says what the situation is today.

Repair. One clause per target: up from, against a current, where none exists today. That last one is legitimate and AERO MIND used it well — "baseline: 0 — no comparable composite indicator exists". A baseline of zero, stated, is a baseline. A baseline unstated is a gap.

Cost: half a day, mostly spent finding out that nobody knows the current figure — which is itself worth discovering before submission.

4 · Activity metrics instead of achievement

What it looks like: the measures count what you will spend rather than what will change.

"The objectives are supported by realistic targets, but they are defined in generic terms (e.g., in terms of total number of tools, total number of interviews)."

NEAR-MISS PIPER — Excellence 3.5, total 13.5

Diagnosis. Twelve interviews is an input. Four tools is an output. Neither tells the evaluator whether anything is different afterwards. Activity metrics are the commonest way a proposal manages to look quantified while measuring nothing.

Repair. For every count, ask "and what does that get us?" Four tools → adopted by how many organisations → assessed how? Keep the count as a milestone if you like. Do not let it be the objective's measure.

5 · The KPI-table displacement

What it looks like: a rich, well-built KPI table three pages after the objectives — and objectives with no numbers in them.

The evidence

KPI density correlates negatively with the ESR total (rho −0.24) and with Excellence (rho −0.26). WON Label2Scale and NEAR-MISS CARDIORISE put threshold, date and owner in the objective text and were both rated STRONG on objectives, at Excellence 5.0 and 4.5. LOST PREDICT carried the densest KPI apparatus in the recent batch, had its objectives passed as "sufficiently measurable, sufficiently verifiable, and sufficiently achievable" — and still scored 3.5 on Excellence, because the method underneath was at family level.

Diagnosis. The information is present but not where the judgement is formed. Excellence is assessed at the top of section 1; your table is in section 2.

Repair. Lift the one decisive number per objective into the objective sentence. Leave the table where it is — it does no harm — but do not make it carry the argument.

Cost: twenty minutes. This is the cheapest item on the list.

6 · The undefined join

What it looks like: the novelty of the project is that two things are brought together, and both things are described beautifully. What is not described is the joint.

"However, some success criteria and validation milestones, including the concordance threshold in the bidirectional validation architecture, are not sufficiently operationalised."

LOST AERO MIND — Excellence 3.5

"There is also insufficient integration between epidemiological, environmental (climate drivers) and socio-economic datasets."

NEAR-MISS AETHER — Excellence 3.5

"In addition, the climate modelling and the linkage between climate and disease data are insufficiently described."

LOST PREDICT — Excellence 3.5

Diagnosis. Four of the nine most recent losses fail here, which makes this the most concentrated failure we found. Both halves get credited; the join is where the novelty actually lives and it is left as the word "integrate".

Repair. If the claim is integration, there must be a number that says what successful integration looks like. Concordance above what? Agreement measured how? Linked at what resolution, with what acceptable loss? Write that number into the objective, and if you cannot produce one, you have found the weak point of the science, not of the writing.

7 · Declared, not demonstrated

What it looks like: a sentence stating that the objectives are measurable, verifiable and achievable — in place of making them so.

Lost · Act4Dem · Excellence 3.0

"A project duration of 42 months, availability of all required expertise in the consortium and a balanced budgeting of the required work tasks ensures achievability of the overall project."

"Therefore, the extent to which some of the objectives can be realistically achieved during the project's lifetime does not clearly arise from the proposal."

Diagnosis. Every proposal claims to be achievable. The claim carries no information. Worse, a paragraph spent asserting it is a paragraph not spent evidencing it, and evaluators notice the substitution.

Repair. Delete the assurance. Replace with one anchor per objective: the prior project that did this at a third of the scale, the cohort already recruited, the platform already at TRL6.

8 · The unowned objective

What it looks like: five objectives, no partner named against any of them; or "the consortium will".

"For instance, it is unclear how the design of a sustainable network mechanism (O5) will be assured."

WON Procure4Health — total 13.5

Diagnosis. Included deliberately as a funded example, because this pattern rarely sinks a proposal on its own — it costs a fraction. But it compounds: an objective with no owner tends also to have no effort behind it, and the reviewer who cannot see who is responsible starts checking whether anyone is.

Repair. One named partner per objective, in the objective line or the row beside it. ALMA did this well — every objective closed with its own "Related to WP(s): …" line naming the work packages that deliver it — and it is one of the reasons its objectives were not criticised for anything except the baseline.

9 · Scope drift and silent coverage

Two failures, one cause: the objectives were written from the consortium inwards rather than from the call outwards.

"The proposed research focuses on autoimmune diseases (AD), which encompass more than 80 pathologies, and therefore is not fully in scope as it does not focus on a single condition. This is a significant weakness."

LOST COMET-AD — Excellence 2.0

"Therefore, the proposal does not satisfactorily respond to the ambition of expected activities in the call topic: 'develop mechanisms and technologies for IPR-aware data manipulation…'"

NEAR-MISS PIPER — Excellence 3.5

Diagnosis. The first is drift — the objectives are about what the consortium studies. The second is silent coverage: the work is probably in there somewhere, but it is not in the objectives, so relevance was judged before the evaluator found it.

Repair. The call-mapping table from Module 2. Build it before you write the objectives, not after. Any expected outcome without an objective beside it is a decision you are making — make it knowingly.

10 · The heroic claim

"The stated objectives are vague, overly ambitious, and at times veer into unscientific territory, such as referring to a 'Holy grail objective' or promising to 'solve the problems once and for all.'"

LOST HEADROOM — total 2.75

Diagnosis. Rare, and instantly fatal when it happens — this proposal took 2.75 out of 5 on the EIC Pathfinder's weighted scale. The mechanism is not that ambition is unwelcome — it is that an unfalsifiable claim tells the evaluator nothing can be checked. Interestingly, we found that winning proposals hedge more than losing ones (3.84 hedging terms per thousand words against 2.59). A qualified claim is a claim an evaluator cannot refute.

Repair. Replace every superlative with the quantity it is standing in for. "Transformative" → what changes, by how much, for whom.

Four rewrites, worked

Read these rather than doing them — the doing comes in Module 6, where the tool marks your work instead of you marking it. What matters here is the reasoning between the two versions.

Rewrite 1 — activity → result

Before · pattern 1

"Identify behavioural, social and environmental determinants of lifestyle behaviours among young people, building on European evidence and participatory research approaches."

After

"Deliver, by M14, a ranked and evidence-graded set of the eight modifiable determinants of physical inactivity and diet in 12–18 year-olds that carry the largest population-attributable fraction, derived from the four named European cohorts and validated against participatory workshops in six sites — replacing the current position in which no cross-country ranking exists. Lead: P4. Related to WP2."

What changed. The verb moved from identify to deliver. Both take a noun; the difference is that identify takes an activity as its object and never completes — you can always identify more determinants — while deliver takes a thing that either exists at the final review or does not. That single change forces every other one: "determinants" had to become a countable, graded set with a stated selection criterion, because you cannot deliver an open-ended category. A baseline appeared, stated explicitly as absent. The population narrowed to something checkable. A date, an owner and a work package attached. Rubric score: 2 on every element.

Rewrite 2 — number → measurement

Before · pattern 3

"Triage accuracy ≥70% vs. clinician gold-standard on a stratified test set by M18."

After

"Raise triage accuracy against clinician gold standard from the 52% achieved by the current paper protocol — measured in the Praia baseline audit at M4 — to ≥70% on a pre-registered stratified test set by M18, with the equity gap between Portuguese and Kriolu flows held below 5 percentage points. Lead: P2, verified by P5. Related to WP4, WP5."

What changed. Only the baseline, honestly. Everything else was already there — and that is the point of showing this one. The original is a good objective missing one clause, and that one clause is what the ESR came back on.

Rewrite 3 — the undefined join

Before · pattern 6

"Integrate climate, epidemiological and socio-economic data to produce a combined risk model."

After

"Link the ERA5-Land climate reanalysis to the three national disease registries at 1 km × daily resolution, accepting no more than 8% of person-time unmatched, and demonstrate by M28 that the linked model improves out-of-sample discrimination over the epidemiological model alone by ≥0.03 C-index in the held-out region. Lead: P7. Related to WP3, WP6."

What changed. "Integrate" was replaced by the actual operation — link, at a stated resolution, with a stated acceptable loss — and by the number that says the integration was worth doing. Note that the second half is the real answer to "why integrate at all": if the combined model is no better than one half of it, the novelty claim collapses.

Rewrite 4 — declared → demonstrated

Before · pattern 7 · Act4Dem, O1 with its justification paragraph, abridged

"O1: Set-up and maintain an effective support and coordination process in the field of democracy and governance … relying on an RRI-based innovation methodology developed, tested and piloted by the H2020 project CHERRIES, coupled with the reliable, contextualizable and scalable LiquidFeedback infrastructure … O1 P/V/A: … A project duration of 42 months, availability of all required expertise in the consortium and a balanced budgeting of the required work tasks ensures achievability of the overall project."

After

"Establish, by M32, the Act4Dem support and coordination process across six demonstrator regions (ES, FI, IT, PT, SE), evidenced by a pooled gain of ≥10 points on the institutional-trust scale measured against each site's own pre-intervention baseline at M8. The method rests on CHERRIES and LiquidFeedback, both already deployed in experimental settings; this is their first replication at six sites under everyday conditions. Lead: P1, evaluation by P6. Related to WP4, WP5."

What changed. The assurance sentence is gone and its space is spent on an anchor — the two named prior deployments, with an explicit statement of what is new about doing it six times under everyday conditions. The endpoint is now a named instrument with a threshold, and the baseline is each site's own earlier measurement, which is both rigorous and cheap. Note that the anchor is honest about its limits: CHERRIES and LiquidFeedback were shown to work in experimental settings, and saying so is stronger than implying more.

The pattern behind the patterns

Eight of these ten failures have the same underlying cause: a decision that has not been made yet. Which model. Which population. Which threshold counts as success. What today's number is. Who owns it.

Which is why writing objectives properly is worth doing early. Done in week two it surfaces the undecided things while there is still time to decide them. Done in the last week it is a formatting exercise, and the ESR will say so.

Module 8 · The tool

The AI prompt generator

Four paths, one for each stage of a proposal. Answer a few questions and the generator compiles a prompt that carries your material and everything this course has taught, ready to paste into whichever assistant you use.

Use on a live proposalNothing is sent anywhereEvery path requires the call topic text

How to get value out of this

  • Pick the stage you are actually at, not the one you wish you were at. The Revision path on a proposal with no agreed result will produce confident nonsense.
  • Paste the whole call topic — expected outcomes, scope, conditions. Every path refuses to proceed without it, for the reason Module 2 gives: relevance is the condition on every other criterion.
  • Give it real numbers. The prompts are written to refuse to invent baselines and prior results, and will return [NEEDED: …] instead. That list is useful output, not a failure.
  • Read what comes back as a draft to argue with. We read plenty of proposals that were confidently wrong.

Using the output well

Argue with it about specificity. The most useful follow-up is almost always the same: "Take objective 2 and push it one further rung down the specificity ladder. Name the method, its inputs and how it will be judged. If that requires a decision nobody has made, say which decision."

Make it defend the numbers. Ask "for every figure in these objectives, tell me where it came from and what happens to the objective if it turns out to be wrong." Anything it cannot source is something you have to source.

Do not let it write the science. The evidence is unambiguous that specificity is what decides, and specificity comes from your technical lead deciding things. A model can tell you that a decision is missing. It cannot make it.

One boundary

The Finalisation path does a consistency check, not an evaluation. It will not predict scores and it will not write you a mock Evaluation Summary Report — that is a separate exercise with its own instrument, and mixing the two produces a document that is neither.

Where you started, and where you are now

The diagnostic in Module 0 asked six questions. Worth going back to it — not to score better, but to notice which of the six you would now answer differently.

The five sentences, once more

  1. Excellence is the gate. No proposal in the 59 proposals was funded below 4.0 on it, and none ever compensated for weak Excellence with strong Implementation.
  2. Within Excellence, the decisive gap is specification — methodology is criticised in 45% of funded proposals and 90% of near-misses.
  3. Weak objectives are a floor risk. But the objective is where under-specification first becomes visible, and it is the cheapest place to find it.
  4. Measurable means target, baseline and verification — and the baseline is the one that is missing.
  5. The call text is the only basis. Everything else is assessed inside it.
